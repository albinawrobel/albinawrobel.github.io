### The quick version

- Your first 10 users come from warm outreach: people who feel the problem right now and have a reason to trust you.
- Work outward in three rings, starting with people you know and ending with strangers who are already looking for a fix.
- A user only counts if they stay past the free trial and are on their way to paying.

When most builders finish building something, they post the link everywhere and wait for strangers to show up. It feels like launching, but in reality it's like throwing a party by taping a flyer to a lamppost and standing by the door. Nobody wants to be the first person at an empty party, and a flyer from someone they've never met isn't going to change their mind. The people who show up first are the ones who got a personal invite, and that's exactly where your first 10 users come from too.

## Why cold outreach rarely gets your first users

Cold outreach fails early on because it asks a stranger to take a risk with nothing to go on. For a user, trying something new means spending time, handing over an email or changing a habit, and doing that for a product nobody they know has used is a gamble with no visible payoff. On top of that, for the founder, a cold launch usually brings silence or a handful of random sign-ups, and neither tells you whether you're solving a real problem.

If you've already tried cold outreach and it went nowhere, it's likely not you; it's the channel. Cold email averages about 45 replies per 1,000 people emailed ([Hunter, 2025](https://hunter.io/the-state-of-cold-email)), and a separate analysis puts closed deals at roughly 1 per 464 emails ([Focus Digital](https://focus-digital.co/cold-email-conversion-rate/)). At that rate, getting 10 customers takes around 4,600 emails.

Personal invites work for the opposite reason: the trust is already there. In Nielsen's 2015 global survey, 83% of people said they trust recommendations from friends and family, the most trusted source in the whole study ([Nielsen](https://www.nielsen.com/global-trust-in-advertising)).

**Takeaway: early users need a reason to trust you before they need a reason to try your product.**

## What warm outreach actually means

Most founders think warm outreach means messaging their friends, but warm is really about intent, not just relationship. A friend who doesn't have the problem will sign up to be nice and then quietly disappear, while a stranger who has been searching for a fix for weeks is a lot warmer than they look. To sort them, ask two questions about every person on your list:

- **Do they feel the problem right now?**
- **Do they have a reason to trust you?**

The best first users get a yes on both. A yes on the problem and a no on trust is still worth a message, because need beats familiarity; however, a no on the problem means they aren't your user yet, no matter how close you are.

**Takeaway: the problem decides who's warm, and the relationship decides how easy the conversation is.**

## Who to reach out to first: the three rings

Think of your outreach as three rings, ordered by how easy the invite is.

- **Inner ring: people you know who have the problem.** Classmates, coworkers, teammates and the people in your group chats. They'll answer your message, and they'll tell you when something is broken.
- **Second ring: people with the problem who are one intro away.** Friends of friends, a mentor's contacts or the people your inner ring works with. The intro does the trust-building for you.
- **Third ring: strangers who are actively looking for a fix.** People asking for recommendations in online communities, complaining about the tool they use now or paying for a clunky workaround. You're new to them, but they're already looking.

Start with the inner ring, even if it's small. Each ring gives you what you need for the next one: your first few users give you proof and introductions, and that turns the second ring into a warm intro instead of a cold message. By the time you reach strangers, you have real users to point to. Instead of asking people to trust your character alone, you can show them something they tend to trust more: data.

**Takeaway: work from the inside out, and use each ring to open the next.**

## Why these rules matter

Now, the obvious pushback is that friends and family will just be nice to you. You're right, they will, which is exactly why the problem comes first and the relationship comes second. Someone without the problem will say it looks great, sign up and never open it again; they're the guest who only comes for the free food. They make the room look fuller, but they can't tell you whether the party is actually any good.

The other pushback is that none of this scales. It doesn't, and it's not supposed to. Ten users is the learning stage, where you find out what people actually use, what confuses them and what they'd pay for. That's much easier to learn from 10 real conversations than from 1,000 anonymous sign-ups, and it's what makes the scalable channels work later.

**Takeaway: at this stage, you want an honest signal, not volume.**

## How to get your first 10 users, step by step

When starting from zero, work through these in order:

1. **Write the problem in one sentence.** It should be specific enough that a stranger reads it and thinks "that's me." "Managing money is hard" is too wide, but "keeping track of shared expenses with roommates" is something people instantly recognize.
2. **List 30 or more people across the three rings.** Rank them by how often they feel the problem and how much it costs them now, whether that's money, time or a messy spreadsheet. Thirty sounds like a lot for 10 users; however, plenty won't reply and some won't actually have the problem.
3. **Message about the problem, not the product.** Ask how they deal with it today, and only bring up what you built if they light up (template below).
4. **Onboard each person by hand.** Get on a call or sit beside them while they set it up, watch where they get stuck and fix it before the next person tries. It's slow, but it's the most useful feedback you'll get at this stage.
5. **Ask what you should keep.** After a week or two, check in with something like: "We're updating a few things soon and I don't want to break what's working. What's been the most useful part for you so far?" People open up a lot more about what they'd miss than they do when asked for a review, and their answer tells you what your product actually is to them. If the answer is good, ask if you can quote them; that's your first testimonial. Then ask if they know anyone else with the same problem.
6. **Count the right people.** A user is someone who stays past the free trial and is on track to pay. Anyone who leaves when the trial ends isn't an actual conversion, so don't count them, but do ask them why they left.

### Outreach template

Swap the bracketed parts for your own audience and keep each message short enough to read in one glance.

**First message (problem only):**

> Hey \[name\], quick question. How are you dealing with \[specific problem\] right now? I keep hearing it's a pain for \[type of person\] and I'm trying to figure out what people actually do about it.

**Follow-up (only if they light up):**

> That's exactly why I built \[product\]. It \[one-line outcome\]. I'm looking for a few people to try it early and tell me what's broken. Want me to set you up?

For the second ring, open with who connected you: "\[mutual contact\] mentioned you deal with \[problem\]." For the third ring, mention where you saw them ask about it, so the message reads like an answer to their question instead of a pitch.

**Takeaway: every first message should start a conversation about the problem; the product comes second.**

## What happens after your first 10 users

Once you have 10 people who stuck around, you have something a cold launch can't give you: proof. Their words become your landing page copy, their intros become your next ring and their reasons for staying tell you which audience to go after next. That's also when broader channels start working, because strangers finally have a reason to trust you that isn't just your word.
