Once founders land their first users, the standard move for getting more is to ask for a referral, and it's usually the weakest way to get one. Asking "can you refer us to a friend?" is like asking your friend to be your wingman by walking up to strangers at a party and listing your best qualities. Technically they can do it, but it's awkward, it feels like work, and the strangers can tell it was your idea.

That's a problem, because word of mouth is one of the strongest channels you'll ever have. A [2021 Nielsen study](https://www.nielsen.com/insights/2021/beyond-martech-building-trust-with-consumers-and-engaging-where-sentiment-is-high/) found that 88% of people trust recommendations from people they know more than any other channel, and an older [McKinsey study](https://www.mckinsey.com/featured-insights/marketing-sales/a-new-way-to-measure-word-of-mouth-marketing) estimated that word of mouth is the main factor behind 20–50% of purchase decisions. On top of that, McKinsey found the most powerful kind comes from someone's own direct experience, which makes up 50–80% of word-of-mouth activity. Your existing users are the ones with that direct experience, so they're sitting on the most valuable marketing you can get; you just can't pull it out of them by asking nicely.

## Why the referral ask backfires

For a user, a referral request turns your product from something they like into something they owe you. Talking about you stops being fun and becomes a chore with your name on it, and people put chores off for as long as humanly possible. For the founder, it also sends a quiet signal you don't want to send: if you have to ask, the user starts to wonder why the product isn't spreading on its own.

The research points somewhere else entirely. The best word of mouth doesn't come from asking users to talk about your product; it comes from giving them a story they actually want to tell about themselves, where your product just happens to show up. Any good wingman will tell you the same thing, because nobody volunteers for the job unless they come off well too. Below are four ways to set that up, in roughly the order they come up in a user's life: while they're using the product, every day after that, when you ask, and once a referral lands.

## Tactic 1: Give them a line about themselves

Nobody wants to wingman for someone when the whole story is about the other person; they want a story where they look good too. Your users work the same way. If you give them a sentence that makes *them* look good, your product becomes the prop in their story, and props get mentioned every time the story gets told.

This works because people already love talking about themselves. Harvard researchers [Diana Tamir and Jason Mitchell (2012)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3361411/) point out that 30–40% of everyday speech is people telling others about their own experiences, and their brain scans showed that talking about yourself activates the brain's reward system. In their experiments, participants gave up an average of 17% of potential earnings just for the chance to talk about themselves, which honestly says more about human nature than any personality test. On top of that, Jonah Berger's [review of word-of-mouth research](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/WOM-Annual-Rev-of-Psych.pdf) names self-enhancement as one of its main drivers: people share things that make them look good, and may hold back things that reflect badly on them.

So don't just deliver a good result; put it into words your user can carry. That might be a weekly recap that tells someone what they got done, a milestone message when they hit something most users don't, or a line from you on a call like "you got set up faster than most teams we work with." Each of these hands the user a small brag about their own progress, and your product comes along every time they repeat it. To be fair, no study has tested this exact move, but the research explains why it works: you're handing users something they already want to say, about the subject they like talking about most.

## Tactic 2: Show up in their everyday conversation

Even the best wingman can only bring you up if the conversation goes somewhere near you. Word of mouth is no different: it comes from being top of mind, not from being exciting, which is good news if your product is a little boring to explain at parties.

[Jonah Berger and Eric Schwartz (2011)](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/10/BzzAgent.pdf) analyzed everyday conversations about more than 300 products. Interesting products got more word of mouth right away, but not more over the following months or overall; however, products that people's surroundings cued more often, or that were more publicly visible, got more word of mouth both immediately and over time. Berger makes the point in his book [*Contagious*](https://www.simonandschuster.ca/books/Contagious/Jonah-Berger/9781451686579) by comparing Disney World with Honey Nut Cheerios. Disney World is far more exciting, but Cheerios wins on ongoing talk because breakfast keeps reminding people it exists. His [review](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/WOM-Annual-Rev-of-Psych.pdf) also points out that people in conversation often just want to fill the space, which is why most of what gets said over lunch isn't remarkable; it's just whatever is on someone's mind.

The lever here is cues, not a new feature. Figure out which moment in your user's week your product actually belongs to, like the Monday planning meeting, the end-of-month invoice scramble or the morning commute, and make sure something reminds them of you right then. The more often their normal routine points back to you, the more chances you get to come up when a friend says "ugh, how do you deal with that?" Visibility counts too, so anything another person sees, like a shared link, an export or a device sitting on a desk, is free airtime. One nuance from [Berger and Raghuram Iyengar](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/?p=11927): being interesting matters more online than face to face, so if most of your users talk about you in person, steady reminders will likely do more for you than one flashy moment.

This is also what keeps your company alive long after launch week. Excitement fades for every product, so if the only reason people talk about you is that you're new, the conversation stops the moment you aren't. However, everyday relevance doesn't wear off, because the routine keeps resetting the reminder: every Monday meeting or invoice deadline is another chance to come up. For a user, a product that never comes up slowly stops feeling necessary, and that's usually how churn starts, long before anyone actually cancels. It's the same reason a wingman who hasn't seen you in months stops bringing you up; nobody forgot you on purpose, you just stopped being part of the conversation.

## Tactic 3: Make the referral a gift, not a favour

There's a big difference between "can you talk me up?" and "you have to meet my friend, you two would get along." The first one is a favour for you, but the second one is a favour for the person being introduced, and it makes the wingman look like a great judge of people. Your referral ask should sound like the second one. Instead of "can you refer us?", try something like "a lot of people don't realize they need this," which lets the user pass on something useful and look good for doing it.

[Rachel Gershon, Cynthia Cryder and Leslie John (2020)](https://source.washu.edu/2020/07/research-shows-how-businesses-can-win-more-customers-influence-your-friends/) tested this across two field experiments and a lab experiment, and found that referral rewards that benefited the *friend* brought in more new customers than rewards for the referrer. The reason was reputation. Customers were about equally likely to refer whether the reward went to them or to their friend, but when referrals were made anonymous, they dropped sharply, because people refer partly to get credit for helping ([the authors' summary](https://kellercenter.hankamer.baylor.edu/news/story/2020/earning-effective-referrals-right-incentives)). Despite this, only 2.6% of the 351 referral programs the researchers surveyed offered friend-only rewards, so most businesses are walking right past it. Wording matters too: [a separate field experiment on a hobby website](https://app.sciencesays.com/p/altruistic-referrals-are-86-more) found that framing the ask altruistically ("give your friend a discount") beat selfish framing for the exact same offer.

Now, there are two fair caveats. These studies tested referral programs with rewards like gift cards and discounts, so they suggest the spoken version works rather than prove it. And "people don't realize they need this" has to actually be true for your product; if it is, you aren't spinning anything, you're just describing the referral accurately. If it isn't, you likely have a positioning problem, and no amount of clever wording will fix that.

## Tactic 4: Thank your wingman in public

When a new user comes in through a referral, say so out loud. Something like "Sam said you'd be a great fit" in the welcome email or on the first call does two things: it reminds the new user how they found you, and it plants an idea that *I should help people the way Sam helped me.* Think of it as thanking your wingman in front of the person they introduced you to; Sam looks good, and the new person learns that introductions are just something people around here do.

[Rachel Gershon and Zhenling Jiang](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/gershon-jiang-2024-express-referral-contagion-downstream-benefits-of-customer-referrals.pdf) found that referred customers make 31–57% more referrals than customers who weren't referred, and that simply reminding referred customers they had been referred raised their referral rate by more than 20% compared with a standard "refer your friends" message. Their proposed reason is that being referred makes referring feel normal and appropriate. Referred customers are also worth more; in a [bank study by Christophe Van den Bulte and colleagues](https://www.ama.org/2019/11/20/maximizing-the-benefits-of-customer-referrals/), referred customers had 16–25% higher lifetime value and were more loyal, partly because of their relationship with the person who referred them.

This ties straight back to Tactic 3. Crediting Sam out loud gives Sam the reputational payoff that made referring worth it in the first place, which makes Sam a lot more likely to do it again.

## Where I would start

If I were starting a new referral program, I wouldn't try all four at once. I would build them in order, because each one makes the next easier:

1. **Find the line.** Look at what your best users have actually achieved with your product, turn it into a sentence about them, and deliver it somewhere they'll see it: a recap, a milestone message or a call.
2. **Find the moment.** Pick the one point in your user's routine your product belongs to, and make sure something reminds them of you there.
3. **Rewrite the ask.** Swap "refer us" for something framed around what the friend gets, and if you offer a reward, test giving it to the friend instead of the referrer.
4. **Close the loop.** Mention the referrer by name to every referred user, and let the referrer know their introduction worked.

The details will look very different for a B2B dashboard than for a consumer app or a piece of hardware, so build each step around how your own ICP talks and where they talk. Copying someone else's referral script just because it worked for them is like borrowing a friend's pickup line; it might be a great line, but it was written for a different crowd.

## Be someone worth talking up

Word of mouth grows when talking about you makes your user look good, and none of this needs a big budget. It just needs you to think about what your user gets to say, not only what you deliver. Treat your users like a wingman you actually want to keep: make the story about them, and thank them in front of everyone when it works.
